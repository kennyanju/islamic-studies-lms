## Introduction: Why Study History and Law?
As a young Muslim entering your teenage years, you are starting to look at the world with a more analytical mind. You are no longer just memorizing stories; you are asking *how* and *why*. 

This handout is designed to match your intellectual curiosity. We will look at three major historical shifts and one practical, real-life question:
1. **The Transition of Prophethood to Legacy:** How did the completion of the Prophet’s ﷺ mission lead to a system of preserving knowledge?
2. **The Caliphate (*Khilāfah*):** How did the early Muslim leaders build an empire while maintaining justice and spiritual focus?
3. **The Maliki School (*Madhhab*):** How did Imam Malik code the practices of Madinah, and how did this jurisprudence shape the history of North and West Africa?
4. **Living Islam Today:** How can you use Islamic theology and law as tools to build your identity and make decisions in a busy, modern world?

## Chapter 1: The Transition of Prophetic Legacy

### 1.1 The Conquest of Makkah (*Fatḥ Makkah*): Mercy as a Political Tool
In the 8th year of the Migration (8 AH), the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ entered the city of Makkah with ten thousand Companions. For twenty years, the leaders of Makkah had persecuted, tortured, and boycotted the Muslims. They had forced them out of their homes and launched multiple military campaigns to destroy them.

Under normal historical standards, a victorious general entering a defeated enemy city would expect plunder, executions, and enslavement. However, the Prophet ﷺ entered Makkah in a state of absolute humility (*Tawāḍu‘*), bowing his head until his beard touched his saddle. 

When he reached the Ka‘bah, he cleansed it of its 360 idols. He then addressed the Quraysh, who stood before him waiting for their fate: *"O Quraysh! What do you think I am about to do with you?"* They replied: *"Good. You are a noble brother, son of a noble brother."* The Prophet ﷺ announced: *"Go, for you are free."* 

He granted a general amnesty, forgiving even those who had killed his beloved family members (like his uncle Hamza). This event, known as the Conquest of Makkah (*Fatḥ Makkah*), demonstrated that the goal of Islam is the reform of hearts and the establishment of peace, rather than physical domination.

---

### 1.2 The Farewell Pilgrimage (*Ḥajjat al-Widā‘*): The Human Rights Charter
In the 10th year of the Hijrah, the Prophet ﷺ led his only pilgrimage after the Migration, known as the Farewell Pilgrimage (*Ḥajjat al-Widā‘*). At Mount ‘Arafāt, he delivered his historic Farewell Sermon (*Khuṭbat al-Widā‘*) to an audience of over 100,000 Companions. This speech was a summary of the social and ethical teachings of Islam:

* **Sanctity of Human Rights:** *"Your blood, your property, and your honor are as sacred as this day of yours, in this month of yours, in this land of yours."*
* **Economic Justice:** The Prophet ﷺ prohibited interest and usury (*Ribā*), which allows the rich to exploit the poor.
* **Racial Equality:** *"All mankind is from Adam and Eve. An Arab has no superiority over a non-Arab, nor does a non-Arab have any superiority over an Arab; also a white has no superiority over a black, nor does a black have any superiority over a white, except by piety (*Taqwā*) and good action."*
* **Gender Justice:** He commanded the community to treat women with kindness, noting that they are partners with mutual rights.

During this pilgrimage, the revelation reached its completion with the verse:
> *"Today I have perfected for you your religion and completed My favor upon you and have approved for you Islam as religion."* (Surah al-Mā’idah, 5:3)

---

### 1.3 The Passing of the Prophet ﷺ and the Concept of Succession
On the 12th of Rabī‘ al-Awwal in the 11th year of the Hijrah, the Prophet ﷺ passed away in Madinah. The grief of the Companions was overwhelming. Sayyiduna ‘Umar, normally a tower of strength, was so shocked that he drew his sword and declared that anyone saying the Prophet ﷺ was dead was a hypocrite.

Sayyiduna Abu Bakr stepped forward. He recited the Quranic verse:
> *"Muhammad is not but a messenger. [Other] messengers have passed on before him. So if he was to die or be killed, would you turn back on your heels? And he who turns back on his heels will never harm Allah at all; but Allah will reward the grateful."* (Surah Al ‘Imran, 3:144)

Abu Bakr’s calm, firm leadership reminded the community that while the Prophet ﷺ was the channel of divine revelation, the object of our worship is the Ever-Living Creator. 

---
> **[Creed Focus: Ash'ari School - The Nature of Prophethood]**
> In Ash'ari creed (*‘Aqīdah*), we emphasize that the Prophet ﷺ completed his role of conveying the message perfectly. With his passing, divine revelation (*Waḥy*) ended. However, we also believe that the Prophets enjoy a special, active life in the intermediate realm (*Barzakh*) where they pray, but their earthly duties of establishing new laws have ceased. This maintains the balance: we love, respect, and follow the Prophet ﷺ, but we worship Allah alone (*Tawḥīd*).
---

The Prophet ﷺ left no wealth. Instead, he left behind a legacy of knowledge. The scholars are described as the heirs of the Prophets (*Warathat al-Anbiyā’*). The core of this legacy is the preservation of the Quran and the Sunnah, which was carried out by the Companions and formalized by the jurists.

## Chapter 2: The Rightly Guided Caliphs (*Al-Khulafā’ al-Rāshidūn*)

After the Prophet's ﷺ passing, the political and administrative leadership of the Ummah passed to the **Rightly Guided Caliphs (*Al-Khulafā’ al-Rāshidūn*)**. The word Caliph (*Khalīfah*) means a deputy or successor. They did not receive revelation, but their job was to protect the religion and govern the state according to Islamic principles.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                       THE AL-KHULAFA' AL-RASHIDUN                           │
├─────────────────────┬───────────────────┬───────────────────┬───────────────┤
│ Abu Bakr al-Siddiq  │ Umar ibn al-Khattab│ Uthman ibn Affan  │ Ali ibn Abi   │
│   (11–13 AH)        │    (13–23 AH)     │    (23–35 AH)     │ Talib (35–40) │
├─────────────────────┼───────────────────┼───────────────────┼───────────────┤
│ • Stabilized state  │ • Formed state    │ • Standardized the│ • Re-established │
│ • Fought apostasy   │   ministries      │   written Quran   │   legal order │
│ • Collected Quran   │ • Created Hijri   │ • Funded mosque   │ • Dealt with  │
│   into first volume │   calendar        │   expansions      │   internal    │
│                     │ • Jerusalem pact  │                   │   conflicts   │
└─────────────────────┴───────────────────┴───────────────────┴───────────────┘
```

### 2.1 Abu Bakr al-Siddiq: The Preserver of Unity (11–13 AH)
Abu Bakr faced immediate threats. Several Arab tribes rebelled, claiming they would no longer pay the obligatory charity (*Zakah*), while false prophets arose to divide the community. 

* **The Fiqh of Zakah:** Abu Bakr declared that he would fight anyone who separated prayer from Zakah. 
* **The Compilation:** Realizing that many Quran memorizers (*Huffāẓ*) were dying in battle, Abu Bakr ordered Zayd ibn Thabit to collect the Quran from palm branches, stones, and the hearts of men, compiling it into a single official volume (*Ṣuḥuf*).

---
> **[Fiqh Focus: Maliki School - Zakah as a Public Duty]**
> In the Maliki school, Zakah is treated as a major social duty. It is not a voluntary charity. The ruler has the right to collect it, and if someone refuses to pay it, the community must enforce it. This ensures that wealth is redistributed to protect the poorest members of society.
---

### 2.2 ‘Umar ibn al-Khattab: The Creator of Institutions (13–23 AH)
‘Umar was an administrative genius who transformed the growing state into an organized society:
* **The Hijri Calendar:** He established the Islamic calendar, using the Migration (*Hijrah*) as the starting point, emphasizing that the Muslim identity is defined by action and community purpose.
* **State Institutions:** He set up ministries (*Dīwān*), a public treasury (*Bayt al-Māl*), and a social welfare system for the needy (both Muslim and non-Muslim).
* **The Opening of Jerusalem:** When the keys of Jerusalem were handed over, ‘Umar walked on foot while his servant rode, showing humility. He signed the Covenant of ‘Umar, ensuring the safety of Christian places of worship, a historical example of religious tolerance.

### 2.3 ‘Uthman ibn ‘Affan: The Standardizer of the Quran (23–35 AH)
‘Uthman ruled a massive state stretching from North Africa to Persia.
* **The Standardized Text (*Al-Muṣḥaf al-‘Uthmānī*):** As non-Arabs entered Islam, variations in reciting the Quran began to cause disputes. ‘Uthman gathered the original pages compiled under Abu Bakr, standardizing the text in the dialect of the Quraysh. He sent certified copies to the provinces and ordered all other variations to be destroyed, preserving the Quran from changes.
* **Martyrdom:** Faced with a violent rebellion by political factions, ‘Uthman refused to use military force in Madinah to protect himself, wanting to avoid shedding Muslim blood. He was assassinated while reading the Quran in his home.

### 2.4 ‘Ali ibn Abi Talib: Navigating Internal Strife (35–40 AH)
‘Ali's rule was marked by political instability and civil conflicts (*Fitnah*). Despite these pressures, he refused to compromise on justice:
* **Government Reform:** He replaced corrupt governors and insisted on complete transparency in the public treasury.
* **Judicial Wisdom:** He was the supreme judge of the Ummah, establishing rules for handling rebel groups and civil conflicts that are still studied in Islamic international law today.

---
> **[Creed Focus: Ash'ari School - Respect for the Companions]**
> In Ash'ari creed, we hold that the conflicts between the Companions (such as the Battle of Camel and Siffin) were based on differences in legal reasoning (*Ijtihād*). We believe that both sides were seeking what they thought was best for the community. We hold our tongues from criticizing them, respecting their status as the generation chosen by Allah to accompany His Prophet ﷺ.

## Chapter 3: Imam Malik, *Amal*, and the Maliki School

To understand how Islamic law is applied, we must study the **Madhhabs** (schools of jurisprudence). A Madhhab is not a sect; it is a system of legal reasoning. One of the four major Sunni schools is the **Maliki Madhhab**, founded by Imam Malik ibn Anas (93–179 AH) in Madinah.

```
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │             SOURCES OF LAW IN THE MALIKI MADHHAB        │
   ├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
   │ 1. The Holy Quran                                       │
   │ 2. The Sunnah (Hadith)                                  │
   │ 3. 'Amal Ahl al-Madīnah (Practice of the People of      │
   │    Madinah)                                             │
   │ 4. Consensus (Ijmā')                                    │
   │ 5. Analogy (Qiyās)                                      │
   │ 6. Public Interest (Maṣlaḥah Mursalah)                  │
   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### 3.1 Who Was Imam Malik?
Imam Malik spent his entire life in Madinah, studying under the children and grandchildren of the Companions. He was known as *Imām Dār al-Hijrah* (The Imam of Madinah). 
* He had extreme respect for the Prophet's ﷺ legacy, refusing to ride an animal in Madinah out of respect for the ground where the Prophet ﷺ lay.
* He compiled the **Muwaṭṭa’** (The Approved Path), which was the first major work combining Hadith and Fiqh. 
* He was highly cautious in giving legal verdicts. He famously remarked: *"The shield of the scholar is to say 'I do not know.' If he neglects it, he will be struck down."*

### 3.2 The Unique Source: *‘Amal Ahl al-Madīnah*
All Sunni schools use the Quran, Sunnah, consensus (*Ijmā‘*), and analogy (*Qiyās*). However, the Maliki school has a unique source of law: **‘Amal Ahl al-Madīnah** (The Practice of the People of Madinah).

Imam Malik argued that the daily, public practices of the people of Madinah were more reliable than solitary reports of Hadith (*Khabar al-Wāḥid*). 
* **The Logic:** If ten thousand people in Madinah did an action (like measuring grain or praying in a certain way) because they saw their parents do it, who saw the Companions do it, who saw the Prophet ﷺ do it, this continuous living practice is more reliable than a single report passed down by one individual.

---
> **[Fiqh Focus: Maliki School - The Posture of Prayer (*Sadl*)]**
> A well-known example of *‘Amal* is the practice of **Sadl** (praying with the arms resting naturally at the sides). While other schools use **Qabḍ** (crossing the hands on the chest or stomach) based on individual Hadiths, the Maliki school holds that *Sadl* is preferred in obligatory prayers (*Farā’iḍ*). 
> 
> In the *Mudawwanah* (the primary collection of Maliki rulings), it is recorded that Imam Malik said he did not know of the major scholars of Madinah crossing their hands in obligatory prayers. This shows that the Maliki school prioritizes the continuous, living practice of the community over isolated written reports.
---

### 3.3 The Travel of Fiqh: From Madinah to Africa
Maliki Fiqh did not stay in Madinah. It spread to become the dominant school in Egypt, North Africa, Al-Andalus (Muslim Spain), and West Africa.

1. **The Moroccan Legacy & al-Qarawiyyīn:** Fatima al-Fihri, a wealthy Muslim woman, founded the **University of al-Qarawiyyīn** in Fez, Morocco, in 859 CE. It became the world's first university and a major hub for Maliki scholarship.
2. **The Saharan Trade Routes:** Islam spread to West Africa through peaceful trade. Muslim merchants from North Africa traveled across the Sahara. Their honesty, fair pricing, and clear contracts—all guided by Maliki commercial law—inspired local empires like Mali and Songhai to embrace Islam.
3. **Timbuktu and Ahmad Baba:** The city of Timbuktu in modern-day Mali became a global center of learning. Ahmad Baba al-Timbukti (d. 1627 CE) was a world-renowned Maliki jurist whose legal verdicts were respected across North Africa.
4. **The Sokoto Caliphate:** In the 18th century, Sheikh Usman dan Fodio led a major educational reform in West Africa, establishing a society run under Maliki law, focusing on women's education and public justice.

## Chapter 4: Living Faith in a Secular Age

As a young Muslim today, you live in a world that is highly connected, fast-paced, and often secular (meaning it separates daily life from God). Balancing your faith, studies, and social life requires understanding how Islam applies to your daily decisions.

### 4.1 The Five Rulings (*Al-Aḥkām al-Khamsah*) as your Compass
Instead of viewing Islamic law as a set of rules designed to restrict you, look at it as a compass. Every action you perform falls into one of five categories:

```
                  ┌────────────────────────────────────────┐
                  │          AL-AHKAM AL-KHAMSAH           │
                  ├───────────┬────────────────────────────┤
                  │   Farḍ    │ Compulsory (e.g., Prayer)  │
                  │  Mandūb   │ Recommended (e.g., Sadaqah)│
                  │  Mubāḥ    │ Permissible (e.g., Hobbies)│
                  │  Makrūh   │ Disliked (e.g., Gossip)    │
                  │  Ḥarām    │ Forbidden (e.g., Stealing) │
                  └───────────┴────────────────────────────┘
```

* **Intention (*Niyyah*):** You can elevate a neutral (*Mubāḥ*) action into a rewardable act of worship by changing your intention. For example, playing video games to relax your mind after a long day of study is *Mubāḥ*. But studying science to help cure diseases for the sake of Allah turns your daily schoolwork into a form of worship (*‘Ibādah*).

---
> **[Creed Focus: Ash'ari School - Human Agency and Allah's Will]**
> In Ash'ari theology, we believe that Allah is the Creator of all causes and effects. However, Allah has given human beings choice and the ability to "acquire" (*Kasb*) their actions. This means you are responsible for the choices you make. We do not say: *"It was written that I fail, so I won't study."* Instead, we work hard and put our trust in Allah (*Tawakkul*), knowing that our effort is our responsibility, and the outcome is created by Allah.
---

### 4.2 Practical Strategy: Balancing Studies and Prayer
One of the most common challenges for teenagers is time management.
* **The Pivot Point:** Do not try to fit your prayers into your busy study and social schedule. Instead, build your schedule around your prayers. 
* **The Benefit:** Structuring your day around the five prayers creates natural breaks for your mind, increases focus, and brings blessing (*Barakah*) into your time.
* **Company (*Ṣuḥbah*):** The Prophet ﷺ said: *"A person is upon the religion of his close friend, so let one of you look to whom he takes as a friend."* Peer pressure is real. Surrounding yourself with friends who value prayer and honesty makes it easier for you to stay strong in your identity.
