## Page 1: Introduction: Establishing the Divine Community

Welcome, young scholar, to this comprehensive study guide for Unit 6. In this unit, we shift our focus from the Makkan period of the Prophetic biography (*Seerah*) to the Madinan period. 

In Makkah, the early Muslims were a persecuted minority. The focus of revelation and practice was primarily on **Tawheed** (the absolute oneness of Allah) and internalizing personal belief (*Aqidah*). The Muslims did not possess a political structure or legal code. 

With the migration (**Hijrah**) to Madinah, the message of Islam matured from personal faith into the establishment of a sacred, model community. In Madinah, the Prophet (pbuh) established:
*   A legal system (*Fiqh*) governing business, family, and international relations.
*   A political covenant (the Constitution of Madinah) managing relations between diverse faith groups.
*   Rules of defensive warfare and ethics of engagement.
*   A system of knowledge transmission that would eventually form the basis of the **Maliki** school of law.

This handout will analyze these developments through the lens of **Ash'ari** theology and **Maliki** jurisprudence. 

### Developing Fiqh Literacy
As you read, pay close attention to:
*   **Ash'ari Creed Focus:** How our theological understanding of destiny (*Qadar*), cause and effect (*Asbab*), and Divine attributes explains the events of Seerah.
*   **Maliki Jurisprudence Focus:** How specific laws, treaties, and rules of engagement were derived by Imam Malik and the early scholars of Madinah.
*   **Arabic Terminology:** Learn the precise vocabulary used by Muslim historians and jurists.

## Page 2: The Hijrah: The Theology of Asbab and Tawakkul

The migration from Makkah to Madinah (known as the **Hijrah** - هِجْرَة) in 622 CE is a watershed moment in Islamic history. So significant was this event that the second Caliph, Umar ibn al-Khattab, established it as the beginning of the Islamic calendar (AH - *Anno Hegirae*).

However, the Hijrah is not merely a historical migration; it is a profound lesson in the relationship between human effort and Divine Will.

> ### Creed Focus: Cause & Effect (Asbab) and Reliance (Tawakkul)
> A core debate in Islamic theology concerns how physical actions relate to outcomes. The **Ash'ari** school teaches a balanced path:
> 
> 1.  **Asbab** (*Asbāb* - أَسْبَاب, singular: *Sabab*): These are the physical causes or worldly means (e.g., eating food to satisfy hunger, preparing weapons for defense, locking a door for safety).
> 2.  **Tawakkul** (*Tawakkul* - تَوَكُّل): This is the absolute trust and reliance of the heart on Allah alone as the Creator of all things.
> 
> **The Ash'ari Rule:** Allah is the sole Creator of both causes and effects. The physical cause does *not* possess the independent, natural power to create the effect. Fire does not burn by its own power; rather, Allah creates the burning *at the moment* the fire touches the wood. 
> 
> Therefore, a Muslim is legally commanded to use the physical means (*Asbab*) out of obedience to Allah's natural laws, but must never rely on those means. 
> *   To rely *only* on the means is a form of hidden association (*Shirk Khafi*), as it attributes creative power to objects.
> *   To ignore the means while claiming "trust in Allah" is foolishness (*Sufah*) and disobedience, as it mocks the order Allah established in the universe.

### The Prophet's Practical Application of Asbab
When the Prophet (pbuh) migrated, he demonstrated perfect Ash'ari theology:
*   He did not simply walk out into the open desert expecting a miracle. He utilized every physical *Sabab* available.
*   He hired an expert guide, Abdullah ibn Urayqit (who was a non-Muslim, proving that worldly expertise should be sought regardless of faith).
*   He hid in the Cave of Thawr for three days until the search parties grew tired.
*   He had Abu Bakr's shepherd, Amir ibn Fuhayrah, drive sheep behind them to erase their tracks.

Yet, when the Quraysh stood at the mouth of the cave, and Abu Bakr feared they would be caught, the Prophet (pbuh) did not look to his physical preparations. He pointed directly to the Ultimate Cause (*Al-Musabbib*): *"O Abu Bakr, what do you think of two, of whom Allah is the third?"* (Quran 9:40).

## Page 3: Sacrifice, Intention (Niyyah), and Brotherhood

To migrate, the Companions had to sacrifice everything. Many left behind spouses, children, houses, and businesses. 

### The Law of Intention (Niyyah)
In Islamic law, the value of an action is determined by its inner motive. This is called **Niyyah** (*Niyyah* - نِيَّة).
The Prophet (pbuh) said in a famous Hadith:
> *"Actions are only by intentions, and every person will have only what they intended. So, whoever's migration (Hijrah) was for Allah and His Messenger, their migration was for Allah and His Messenger. And whoever's migration was for some worldly gain or to marry a woman, their migration was for that which they migrated."* (Sahih al-Bukhari & Muslim).

This Hadith teaches that two people can perform the exact same physical action (traveling 280 miles through the desert), yet one receives the highest reward from Allah, while the other receives nothing, because of their internal *Niyyah*.

### Reconstructing Society: The Muwakhah
Upon arriving in Yathrib—which the Prophet (pbuh) renamed **Al-Madinah al-Munawwarah** (The Illuminated City)—he faced a massive refugee crisis. The Emigrants (**Al-Muhajirun** - الْمُهَاجِرُونَ) had arrived with no capital, while the local helpers (**Al-Ansar** - الأَنْصَار) owned the agricultural lands.

To solve this, the Prophet (pbuh) established the **Muwakhah** (*Muwākhāh* - الْمُؤَاخَاة) — a formal, legally binding covenant of brotherhood pairing one Muhajir with one Ansari. 
*   The Ansar offered to divide their wealth, lands, and homes in half.
*   The Muhajirun accepted this brotherhood but maintained their economic dignity. Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf, when offered half of his Ansari brother's wealth, replied: *"May Allah bless your wealth and your family. Just show me the way to the market."* Within a short time, he became one of the wealthiest merchants in Madinah.

## Page 4: Wathiqat al-Madinah (The Constitution)

Madinah was not a homogeneous town; it was a complex tribal society composed of two major pagan Arab tribes (Aws and Khazraj), three major Jewish tribes (Banu Qaynuqa', Banu al-Nadir, and Banu Qurayzah), and the newly arrived Makkan Muslims.

To govern this diverse society, the Prophet (pbuh) drafted **Wathiqat al-Madinah** (*Wathīqat al-Madīnah* - وَثِيقَة الْمَدِينَة) — the Constitution of Madinah. Written in 622 CE, it is recognized by historians as the first written constitution in human history.

```
       +-----------------------------------------------------------+
       |             THE CIVIC MATRIX OF THE CONSTITUTION          |
       +-----------------------------------------------------------+
                                     |
         +---------------------------+---------------------------+
         |                                                       |
         v                                                       v
+------------------+                                    +------------------+
|   CIVIC RIGHTS   |                                    |   CIVIC DUTIES   |
| * Freedom of     |                                    | * Shared defense |
|   Religion       |                                    |   of the city    |
| * Absolute equal |                                    | * No alliances   |
|   justice        |                                    |   with enemies   |
| * Protection of  |                                    | * Collective tax |
|   property       |                                    |   for war costs  |
+------------------+                                    +------------------+
```

### The Concept of Ummah in the Constitution
Crucially, the Constitution declared that the Muslims and the Jewish tribes who signed the agreement formed **Ummah Wahidah** (*Ummah Wāḥidah* - أُمَّة وَاحِدَة) — a single, unified civic community. 
*   *"The Jews of Banu Awf are a community (Ummah) alongside the believers. The Jews have their religion, and the Muslims have theirs."*
*   This document demonstrates that a state can be Islamic in its authority, yet pluralistic and protective of non-Muslim citizens' rights.

## Page 5: Fiqh of Safety (Aman) and Treaties ('Ahd)

In the **Maliki** school of jurisprudence, the laws of international relations and treaties are based directly on the historical actions of the Prophet (pbuh) in Madinah. Imam Malik codified these rules under two major categories:

### 1. The Legality of Aman (Safety)
**Aman** (*Amān* - أَمَان) is the granting of asylum or safe passage to a non-Muslim. 
*   **Maliki Ruling:** In the Maliki school, *any* free Muslim citizen—whether male or female, rich or poor—has the legal authority to grant *Aman* to a non-Muslim traveler or diplomat. Once a citizen grants safety, the entire state and the army are legally bound to protect that person.
*   Imam Malik ruled that even if a Muslim merely points or makes a physical gesture of peace that a non-Muslim interprets as safety, the *Aman* is valid and legally binding. 

### 2. The Sanctity of 'Ahd (Covenant)
**'Ahd** (*'Ahd* - عَهْد) refers to a treaty or peace agreement. 
*   **Maliki Ruling:** Breaking a treaty is classified as a major sin and a legal crime. If a treaty is active between the Islamic state and another community, Muslims are strictly forbidden from committing **Ghadr** (*Ghadr* - غَدْر) — treachery or surprise attacks.
*   If the state fears that the other side is about to betray the treaty, they cannot attack them secretly. The Quran commands that the treaty must be formally dissolved first, so both sides are on equal terms: *"And if you fear treachery from a people, throw back [their covenant] to them on equal terms. Indeed, Allah does not love the treacherous"* (Quran 8:58).

## Page 6: Badr & Uhud: Decree, Failure, and Patience

For thirteen years in Makkah, Muslims were forbidden from physical combat (*Qital*). They were commanded to control their hands and establish prayer. In Madinah, after being driven from their homes and losing their properties, Allah revealed the permission to fight back defensively:
> *"Permission [to fight] has been given to those who are being fought, because they were wronged. And indeed, Allah is competent to give them victory."* (Quran 22:39).

This sets the stage for the battles of Badr and Uhud, which were tests of spiritual discipline rather than struggles for power.

### Badr (2 AH): The Test of Certainty (Yaqeen)
At Badr, the Muslims were severely outnumbered (313 against 1,000). 
*   **Aqidah Connection:** The victory at Badr was a demonstration of Divine Will. Allah sent angels, not because He needed them, but to bring **Sakinah** (tranquility) to the hearts of the believers. 
*   The Quranic verse: *"And you threw not when you threw, but it was Allah who threw..."* (Quran 8:17) explains that while human acquisition (*Kasb*) performs the action, the ultimate creation of the effect belongs to Allah.

### Uhud (3 AH): The Test of Obedience
At Uhud, the Prophet (pbuh) placed 50 archers on Mount Aynayn to guard the army's rear. He gave them an absolute command not to leave under any circumstances. 
*   When the battle initially went in favor of the Muslims, the archers saw the physical spoils of war (**Ghanimah** - غَنِيمَة). Tempted by worldly wealth (*Dunya*), they disobeyed the command and ran down the hill.
*   The Quraysh cavalry seized this opening, circled the mountain, and attacked from behind, resulting in heavy losses for the Muslims and the wounding of the Prophet (pbuh) himself.

**The Theological Lesson:** Uhud proved that physical preparation without spiritual obedience leads to disaster. It also demonstrated the human nature of the Prophet (pbuh)—he could be wounded and bleed—showing that he is a messenger of Allah, not a divine entity.

## Page 7: Just War Theory & the Maliki Military Code

Islamic jurisprudence does not glorify war. War is viewed as a painful necessity to remove tyranny and protect religious freedom. The **Maliki** school contains some of the strictest rules in Islamic history regarding the ethics of conflict, derived directly from the Prophetic Sunnah and the commands of the first Caliph, Abu Bakr.

### The Maliki Code of Combat:
In his book *Al-Muwatta*, Imam Malik recorded the instructions given to the Muslim armies. These rules form the basis of Islamic Just War Theory:

1.  **Sanctity of Non-Combatants:** It is strictly prohibited to target or kill women, children, the elderly, the sick, and disabled persons.
2.  **Protection of Religious Freedom:** Monks, priests, and anyone dedicating themselves to worship in their temples, churches, or synagogues must be left completely alone. Their places of worship cannot be damaged.
3.  **Sanctity of Nature:** Cut down no fruitful trees, burn no fields of grain, and do not destroy crops.
4.  **No Scorched-Earth Policies:** Do not ruin wells, poison water supplies, or destroy buildings, houses, or towns.
5.  **Sanctity of Animals:** Do not slaughter sheep, cows, or camels except for what is necessary for food. Killing animals to ruin the enemy's economy is strictly forbidden.

### Comparison with Modern History
These rules were codified in Madinah in the 2nd century Hijri (8th century CE). By contrast, modern international laws, such as the Geneva Conventions, were only formulated in the mid-20th century. Islam established environmental protection and human rights in warfare over a thousand years prior.

## Page 8: The Farewell Sermon & the Five Necessities

In 10 AH (632 CE), the Prophet (pbuh) led his final pilgrimage, known as **Hajj al-Wada'** (*Ḥajj al-Wadā'* - حَجّ الْوَدَاع). At Mount Arafat, he delivered his historic Farewell Sermon (**Khutbat al-Wada'** - خُطْبَة الْوَدَاع) to over 100,000 companions.

This sermon served as a grand summary of the social, economic, and moral laws of Islam.

### The Five Necessities (Al-Daruriyyat al-Khams)
Islamic jurists, particularly in the Maliki school, note that this sermon established the protection of the **Five Necessities** (*Al-Ḍarūriyyāt al-Khams* - الضَّرُورِيَّات الْخَمْس), which are the core objectives of the Shariah (*Maqasid*):

1.  **Preservation of Life (*Hifz al-Nafs*):** The Prophet (pbuh) declared: *"Indeed, your blood and your property are sacred to you, like the sacredness of this day of yours, in this month of yours, in this city of yours."*
2.  **Preservation of Wealth (*Hifz al-Mal*):** He explicitly prohibited **Riba** (*Ribā* - رِبًا) — usury/interest. He declared all past usury canceled, starting with that of his uncle, Abbas. This stopped rich lenders from exploiting poor debtors, protecting economic wealth.
3.  **Preservation of Lineage and Family (*Hifz al-Nasl*):** He commanded men to treat women with absolute justice: *"Fear Allah concerning women, for you have taken them as a trust (Amanah) from Allah."*
4.  **Preservation of Honor (*Hifz al-'Ird*):** He forbade slander and tribalism, declaring that all human beings are descendants of Adam, and Adam was from dust.
5.  **Preservation of Religion (*Hifz al-Din*):** He urged the Muslims to hold fast to the Quran and Sunnah, warning them that while the devil could no longer make them worship idols, he would try to ruin their faith through small, division-causing sins.

## Page 9: How Knowledge Traveled: Amal Ahl al-Madinah

How did these legal rulings and historical facts reach us with such precision? 

In the **Maliki** school, the answer lies in a unique methodology called **'Amal Ahl al-Madinah** (*'Amal Ahl al-Madīnah* - عَمَل أَهْل الْمَدِينَة) — the Practice of the People of Madinah.

```
+-------------------------------------------------------------+
|             THE GOLDEN CHAIN OF TRANSMISSION                |
+-------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                                             |
|   1. THE SOURCE: Prophet Muhammad (pbuh)                    |
|      (Lived, ruled, and taught in Madinah for 10 years)     |
|                               |                             |
|                               v                             |
|   2. THE COMPANIONS: Ibn Umar, Aisha, Zayd ibn Thabit       |
|      (Observed the Sunnah daily and taught the city)        |
|                               |                             |
|                               v                             |
|   3. THE TABI'UN: The Seven Fiqh Scholars / Nafi'           |
|      (Consolidated the laws and practices of the Sahabah)   |
|                               |                             |
|                               v                             |
|   4. THE COMPILER: Imam Malik ibn Anas                      |
|      (Codified these practices into the Maliki Madhhab)     |
+-------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### Why Imam Malik Valued 'Amal
Imam Malik argued that the continuous, daily practice of the people of Madinah was more reliable than a single written report (*Khabar al-Wahid*). 
His logic was:
*   If 10,000 people in a city are performing an action (such as letting their arms hang naturally in prayer—**Sadl**, or calling the Adhan in a specific way) because they saw their fathers do it, who saw the Sahabah do it, who saw the Prophet (pbuh) do it, this is a mass-transmitted, living Sunnah (*Mutawatir*).
*   A written report from one person might contain a mistake, but a whole city of scholars cannot collectively forget how the Prophet (pbuh) prayed and lived among them.

## Page 10: Synthesis & Critical Engagement

By studying the Seerah in Madinah, we learn that building a community requires:
1.  **Spiritual Alignment:** Acting upon physical means (*Asbab*) while maintaining absolute trust (*Tawakkul*) in Allah.
2.  **Civic Cooperation:** Developing covenants of peace and justice with people of other backgrounds.
3.  **Spiritual Discipline:** Understanding that patience (*Sabr*) and obedience are the roots of victory.
4.  **Scientific Inheritance:** Valuing the living chain of knowledge passed down through generations.
